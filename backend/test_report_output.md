# BIOENERGY-POLICY

## 1. Summary of the Impact

Research and policy-facing expertise led by Patricia Thornley, the Supergen Bioenergy Hub and EBRI informed UK policy on sustainable bioenergy, low-carbon fuels and BECCS. The strongest evidenced impacts are on national policy and advisory processes used by the Committee on Climate Change (CCC), DESNZ and related cross-Whitehall teams. Thornley was acknowledged as an expert contributor to the CCC's 2011 *Bioenergy Review*, whose advice on the first three carbon budgets was accepted by Government and whose recommended fourth carbon budget "was legislated in June 2011" [1]. From August 2020 onward, Supergen and Aston-linked work fed directly into the 2023 UK *Biomass Strategy*, including development of a stronger sustainability framework for biomass and BECCS [4][5][6]. The policy reach is national, spanning electricity, heat and transport fuels across 173.5TWh of UK biomass use in 2022 [4].

## 2. Underpinning Research

The underpinning research is a body of work on bioenergy systems analysis, lifecycle assessment (LCA), greenhouse-gas accounting, biomass sustainability criteria, biomass resource availability and the strategic role of BECCS in net-zero pathways. The evidence supports three main research strands.

First, Thornley's long-running expertise in bioenergy lifecycle and sustainability assessment was sufficiently established by 2011 for the CCC to acknowledge her as providing "significant support" to its *Bioenergy Review* [1, Acknowledgements, p.5]. That review addressed sustainable bioenergy, lifecycle emissions accounting and the role of bioenergy in meeting UK carbon budgets. However, this 2011 contribution predates the supplied Aston affiliation window. Patricia Thornley is stated here to have been affiliated with Aston University from 2015 to present. Therefore, the 2011 underpinning expertise should currently be treated as **individual pre-Aston research contribution; attribution to Aston needs verification**.

Second, the evidence identifies formal research and synthesis on policy-relevant bioenergy modelling and sustainability standards during the REF underpinning period. The clearest named output is Welfle, Thornley and Roeder, "A review of the role of bioenergy modelling in renewable research & policy development" in *Biomass and Bioenergy* (2020), cited in the IEA Bioenergy Task 33 UK update as part of work on "Developing Enabling Policy Frameworks" [2]. Although the extracted evidence does not provide the DOI, journal pages or publication metadata, it does identify the output title and venue. This is potentially important underpinning research because it links modelling methods directly to policy development. Since 2020 falls within Thornley's Aston affiliation window (2015-present), this output is **likely to include Aston-attributable underpinning research**, but the exact institutional affiliations on the paper need to be checked and supplied.

Third, the Supergen Bioenergy Hub produced policy-facing analytical outputs on greenhouse-gas and sustainability standards across transport, heat and power. The November 2020 briefing note *GHG Sustainability Standards* synthesised UK and EU rules and identified inconsistencies across sectors [3]. This is not a peer-reviewed paper, but it is a formal research-quality policy output grounded in comparative analysis of sustainability regulation. It contains quantified sector context, including: "About 4% of the UK's transport fuels are supplied from renewable fuels" [3, section "Bioenergy in the UK"]. Because this briefing was published in 2020, it falls within Thornley's Aston period. However, the extracted evidence does not state authorship, grant support or whether Thornley was a named author; those details need to be supplied to secure Aston attribution.

The evidence also points to a Supergen sustainability criteria review and biomass feedstock analysis used in relation to the 2023 *Biomass Strategy* [4][6]. These appear to be substantive underpinning analyses on land use, domestic biomass supply, perennial energy crops and resource availability. However, in the current evidence pack, the existence and policy use of these analyses are described mainly through a Supergen news item and a Carbon Brief article rather than through independently hosted research outputs. That is an **evidence gap** for Section 2. For REF submission, the academic team should supply the underlying reports themselves, with publication dates, authors, institutional affiliations and, ideally, persistent URLs.

### Aston's institutional role versus individual role

- **Patricia Thornley (Aston University, 2015-present):**
  - **At Aston:** 2020 onward underpinning work on bioenergy modelling, sustainability standards, biomass resource availability and policy frameworks appears to fall within her Aston period [2][3][6].
  - **Before Aston / not yet attributable to Aston:** her acknowledged contribution to the CCC *Bioenergy Review* in 2011 predates the supplied Aston window and should not be credited to Aston without further evidence [1].

- **Supergen Bioenergy Hub / EBRI:**
  - The evidence links Thornley to leadership roles in both Supergen and EBRI [2], and Carbon Brief describes the Supergen Bioenergy Hub as "Aston University-based" in the 2023 policy context [4]. That helps distinguish Aston's institutional role in later impact.
  - However, the evidence does not yet provide formal documentation of when Aston became host institution for the relevant Supergen work, nor whether all cited Supergen outputs were authored under Aston's institutional lead. This needs verification.

To make the case REF-ready, the academic team should supply:

2. The underlying Supergen sustainability review and biomass feedstock/resource reports cited as informing the 2023 *Biomass Strategy* [6], hosted independently if possible.

4. Any grant numbers, funders or award values for Supergen/EBRI work relevant to these outputs. None are stated in the extracted evidence, so they cannot be claimed here.

## 4. Details of the Impact

### Pathway from Research to Impact

The pathway is clear in outline, though some links still need stronger documentary proof.

Research on bioenergy LCA, sustainability criteria, biomass resource availability and BECCS created a policy-relevant evidence base [1][2][3]. Thornley and Supergen then translated that research into advisory and synthesis formats suited to government use: CCC review support [1], policy-framework work for BEIS/DfT [2], a formal briefing on greenhouse-gas and sustainability standards [3], and later direct engagement with DESNZ on the *Biomass Strategy* [6].

That translation route mattered because UK bioenergy policy cuts across electricity, heat, transport fuels and greenhouse-gas removals. The research did not remain in academic journals. It was carried into government through expert advisory roles, targeted reports, webinars and strategy-facing evidence [2][6]. By 2023, this route had produced documented policy uptake: DESNZ's *Biomass Strategy* linked to and reflected evidence on sustainability and BECCS, and committed to "developing this framework, and to consult on the details in 2024" [5].

### Reach

The evidence shows impact across at least seven named public-policy beneficiaries and intermediaries in the UK: the Committee on Climate Change, DESNZ, BEIS, DfT, Parliament, Sciencewise and cross-Whitehall policy teams [1][2][5][6].

The reach is national. The earliest documented policy reach is through the CCC, the UK's statutory climate adviser. Its *Bioenergy Review* states that advice on the first three carbon budgets was accepted by Government and that "the fourth carbon budget was legislated in June 2011 at the level that we recommended" [1, Preface, p.2]. Thornley is named in the acknowledgements as one of the individuals providing significant support [1, Acknowledgements, p.5]. That places her expertise inside a policy process affecting UK-wide carbon-budget legislation.

The later and stronger REF-eligible impact evidence is concentrated in the 2020-2024 period. The IEA Bioenergy Task 33 UK update records Supergen work on "Developing Enabling Policy Frameworks" and specifically notes a "Forthcoming Supergen Bioenergy Hub report and webinar on biomass resource availability for BEIS/DfT" [2]. This demonstrates direct reach into the two central UK departments responsible for energy and transport decarbonisation.

The 2020 Supergen briefing note also shows the breadth of the policy field being addressed. It states that "About 4% of the UK's transport fuels are supplied from renewable fuels" [3], indicating relevance to the Renewable Transport Fuel Obligation and low-carbon fuels policy as well as to heat and power.

The broadest documented reach comes through the 2023 *Biomass Strategy*. Carbon Brief reports that the strategy addressed a biomass system amounting to 173.5TWh in 2022, including 62.2TWh for electricity, 32.7TWh for heat and 27.3TWh for transport fuels [4]. This means the policy influence was not confined to one niche technology. It reached multiple sectors of the UK energy system.

The Sciencewise evaluation adds process reach. The DESNZ/BEIS public dialogue on biomass and BECCS involved about 105 public participants, with 95 attending all five workshops, and findings were presented to 38 policy makers at a cross-Whitehall webinar in February 2023 [5]. Aston-linked relevance is present through Oversight Group leadership by Dr Mirjam Roder of Aston University, though the current case focus remains Thornley/Supergen/EBRI.

### Significance

**Public policy impact:** The strongest significance claim is influence on formal UK policy frameworks. The CCC review fed into carbon-budget advice accepted by Government, with the fourth carbon budget legislated at the level recommended by the CCC [1]. In the later REF-eligible period, DESNZ's *Biomass Strategy* incorporated evidence on sustainability and feedstocks and committed to a stronger sustainability framework for all biomass types, including BECCS [5][6]. This is a substantive policy change because it moves from fragmented sector-specific rules toward a cross-sector framework.

**Environmental impact:** The policy issues addressed are central to UK net zero. The IEA Bioenergy Task 33 update states: "In the UK it is envisaged that BECCS could deliver c.-55 million tonnes of net negative emissions per annum... and reduce the cost of meeting the UK's 2050 GHG emissions target by up to 1% of GDP" [2]. This does not by itself prove Thornley caused that policy position, but it demonstrates the environmental and strategic significance of the policy area in which her research was being used.

**Energy-system impact:** The 2023 strategy affected a nationally significant resource base. Carbon Brief reports biomass supplied 8.6% of UK energy in 2022 and details 173.5TWh of biomass use across electricity, heat and transport [4]. Influence on the sustainability rules governing that scale of activity is significant because it shapes future deployment, feedstock sourcing and carbon accounting across several sectors.

**Transport and low-carbon fuels impact:** The Supergen sustainability standards briefing addressed policy inconsistencies across transport, heat and power, in a context where renewable fuels already supplied about 4% of UK transport fuels [3]. This matters because low-carbon fuels policy depends on robust lifecycle and sustainability standards to avoid perverse incentives and ensure fair treatment across sectors.

**Democratic and public-services impact:** The Sciencewise evaluation shows that evidence on biomass and BECCS was not only used internally by DESNZ but also tested through structured public dialogue and then fed back to 38 policy makers [5]. Publication of the dialogue report alongside the *Biomass Strategy* on 10 August 2023 indicates that the evidence informed a live government decision process [5].

Across these strands, the significance lies less in a single attributable regulation than in shaping the UK's governing framework for sustainable biomass, low-carbon fuels and BECCS at national scale. Two additional corroborating sources [4][6] support the same conclusion, though stronger beneficiary letters would materially improve the case.

### Attribution to the Research

The evidence supports a **contribution claim** rather than sole causation.

What is evidenced:
- Thornley was acknowledged by the CCC as an expert contributor to the 2011 *Bioenergy Review* [1].
- Supergen was engaged in policy-framework development for BEIS and DfT in 2020 [2].
- Supergen states that it "worked closely with teams from the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero and other government departments to provide scientific evidence, context and insight to inform the strategy" [6].
- The Sciencewise evaluation confirms that the 2023 strategy committed to a stronger sustainability framework and that the dialogue report was published alongside the strategy [5].
- Carbon Brief independently links Thornley and the Aston University-based Supergen Bioenergy Hub to the biomass strategy process [4].

What still needs corroboration:
- Exact citations from the 2023 *Biomass Strategy* to specific Thornley/Supergen outputs are not quoted in the current evidence pack. The Supergen news item says previous work "is referenced in the strategy" [6], but the exact footnote text and page numbers from the government strategy are not supplied. This is a key gap.
- The current evidence does not provide a direct DfT or DESNZ statement confirming how Thornley/Supergen evidence changed policy wording, consultation design or implementation decisions.
- The 2011 CCC impact predates Thornley's Aston affiliation and therefore cannot currently be claimed as Aston impact without careful framing as background to later Aston-based work.

**Patricia Thornley (Aston University, 2015-present):**
- Impacts from 2020 onward, including Supergen work for BEIS/DfT and the 2023 *Biomass Strategy* process, occurred during her Aston affiliation window [2][4][6].
- The 2011 CCC contribution occurred before the supplied Aston window and should be presented as pre-Aston expertise informing her later Aston-based policy role, not as Aston-owned impact [1].

No other named researcher has an affiliation window supplied here, so institutional attribution beyond Thornley should be treated cautiously.
