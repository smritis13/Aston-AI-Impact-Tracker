# BIOENERGY-POLICY

## 1. Summary of the Impact

Research and policy-facing analysis led by Patricia Thornley, the Supergen Bioenergy Hub and Aston-linked bioenergy expertise shaped UK policy on sustainable biomass, low-carbon fuels and BECCS. The strongest evidenced impacts are on national policy formation by the Committee on Climate Change (CCC) and the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ), including the 2023 UK Biomass Strategy and earlier carbon-budget advice [1][5][6]. Reach is national: the policy framework applies across electricity, heat, transport fuels and emerging biomass-using sectors, in a system where UK biomass use reached 173.5TWh in 2022 and biomass supplied 8.6% of UK energy [4]. Significance is shown by legislated carbon budgets [1], DESNZ's commitment to develop a stronger sustainability framework for all biomass including BECCS [5], and direct engagement with BEIS/DfT on biomass resource availability and policy mechanisms [2].

## 2. Underpinning Research

The underpinning research is a body of work on bioenergy systems, lifecycle assessment (LCA), greenhouse-gas accounting, sustainability criteria, biomass resource availability and the role of BECCS in UK decarbonisation. The evidence supports three main research strands.

First, Thornley's expertise contributed to national analysis of sustainable bioenergy and lifecycle emissions accounting. The CCC's **Bioenergy Review** (2011) is not itself a peer-reviewed output, but it is a formal policy-quality document showing that Thornley's expertise was drawn into the UK's statutory climate-advice process. The report records that CCC advice on the first three carbon budgets "was accepted by Government and legislated by Parliament" and that "the fourth carbon budget was legislated in June 2011 at the level that we recommended" (Preface, p.2) [1]. Thornley is named in the acknowledgements as having provided significant support (Acknowledgements, p.5) [1]. This is strong evidence of research-informed expert input, but not on its own a complete underpinning-research reference; the academic team should supply the specific research outputs on LCA/sustainability that informed Thornley's contribution.

Second, the Supergen Bioenergy Hub produced formal synthesis and briefing outputs on biomass sustainability and cross-sector policy coherence. The **GHG Sustainability Standards Briefing Note** (November 2020) is a substantive policy-facing research output, not merely publicity. It synthesises UK and EU sustainability rules across transport, heat and power, and identifies inconsistencies in carbon accounting and sustainability governance [3]. The note quantifies the policy context: "About 4% of the UK's transport fuels are supplied from renewable fuels" and two-thirds of UK renewable energy in 2018 came from bioenergy (section "Bioenergy in the UK") [3]. This matters because the later policy impact on DESNZ's cross-sector sustainability framework is directly about harmonising criteria across those same sectors [4][5]. However, the extracted evidence does not provide named authors, DOI, or an independently hosted version beyond the Supergen PDF; the academic team should provide full bibliographic details and authorship.

Third, the evidence points to a more formal research base on modelling and resource analysis. The IEA Bioenergy Task 33 UK update identifies a linked research output: **Welfle, Thornley & Roeder, "A review of the role of bioenergy modelling in renewable research & policy development" (Biomass and Bioenergy, 2020)** [2]. This appears to be the strongest peer-reviewed underpinning output named in the evidence because it connects directly to policy development and biomass resource analysis for government. However, the extracted evidence does not include the DOI, journal volume/pages, or a URL to the paper itself. That is an evidence gap that should be closed before submission. The same source also situates Thornley as Director of EBRI and Director of the Supergen Bioenergy Hub in work on "Developing Enabling Policy Frameworks" and a "Forthcoming Supergen Bioenergy Hub report and webinar on biomass resource availability for BEIS/DfT" [2]. This supports the existence of a research-to-policy translation pipeline, but the actual report/webinar materials and any departmental uptake evidence need to be supplied.

A further underpinning strand concerns BECCS. The IEA update cites CCC and ETI evidence that BECCS could deliver "c.-55 million tonnes of net negative emissions per annum" and reduce the cost of meeting the UK's 2050 target by "up to 1% of GDP" [2]. This is important context for the significance of the research area, but the extracted evidence does not prove that these estimates were generated by Aston-based research. The academic team should therefore provide the specific Thornley/Supergen/EBRI outputs on BECCS systems analysis, sustainability or deployment pathways that were used in policy engagement.

### Aston attribution and affiliation

Aston attribution needs careful handling. The only affiliation window supplied is a **reported, not independently confirmed** lead: Patricia Thornley "joined Aston University in 2015 as Professor of Bioenergy, having previously been at the University of Manchester" (LinkedIn search result). This should **not** be stated as fact without confirmation by the academic team.

On the evidence currently available:

- **2011 CCC Bioenergy Review**: Thornley's contribution predates the reported 2015 Aston start, so this should presently be treated as **not attributable to Aston unless the team can evidence an Aston institutional role or a different confirmed affiliation timeline** [1].
- **2020 onward Supergen/EBRI/DESNZ/DfT activity**: this falls within the likely Aston period if the reported 2015 date is confirmed, so these impacts are more plausibly attributable to Aston [2][3][5][6].
- **Supergen Bioenergy Hub institutional base**: one independent source describes the Hub as "Aston University-based" [4]. That is useful corroboration for Aston's institutional role in the 2023 policy window, but the underlying governance documents or grant records should be supplied for REF submission.

## 4. Details of the Impact

### Pathway from Research to Impact

The pathway is clear in broad terms.

Research on bioenergy systems, lifecycle emissions, sustainability criteria and biomass resource availability was translated into policy-facing reviews, briefing notes, expert advice and direct engagement with government departments [1][2][3][6]. Thornley's expertise first entered the UK climate-policy system through the CCC's Bioenergy Review, which informed advice on carbon budgets and sustainable bioenergy [1]. That early role established credibility in national policy debates on lifecycle accounting, sustainability regulation and the strategic role of BECCS.

From 2020 onward, the Supergen Bioenergy Hub and associated Aston-linked expertise moved from advisory contribution to more structured policy engagement. The Hub produced synthesis on greenhouse-gas and sustainability standards across transport, heat and power [3]; worked with BEIS and DfT on biomass resource availability and policy mechanisms [2]; and, by DESNZ's own policy process, fed evidence into the 2023 Biomass Strategy [5][6]. The route to impact was therefore: research and modelling -> synthesis/briefing and expert-group participation -> departmental engagement -> incorporation into national strategy and consultation commitments.

The strongest evidenced adoption point is the 2023 Biomass Strategy. DESNZ's associated public-dialogue evaluation states that the dialogue findings were presented to 38 policymakers in a cross-Whitehall webinar in February 2023 and that the dialogue report was published alongside the Biomass Strategy on 10 August 2023 [5]. Supergen states that it "worked closely with teams from the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero and other government departments to provide scientific evidence, context and insight to inform the strategy" [6]. Independent reporting then links the strategy's sustainability framework to an Aston University-based Supergen review and to Thornley's expert-group role [4].

### Reach

The impact reached at least six named beneficiary organisations and policy arenas across UK government, statutory advisory bodies and cross-Whitehall policy processes: DESNZ, BEIS, DfT, the CCC, Parliament and Sciencewise-supported public-policy structures [1][2][5][6].

The primary reach is national. The CCC's bioenergy advice fed into UK carbon-budget governance, with the report stating that advice on the first three carbon budgets "was accepted by Government and legislated by Parliament" and that the fourth carbon budget "was legislated in June 2011 at the level that we recommended" [1]. That places Thornley-linked expertise inside a policy process affecting the whole UK economy.

The 2023 Biomass Strategy widened that reach across multiple sectors. Independent reporting on the strategy states that UK biomass use in 2022 totalled **173.5TWh**, including **62.2TWh for electricity**, **32.7TWh for heat** and **27.3TWh for transport fuels** [4]. The same source reports that biomass supplied **8.6% of UK energy** in 2022 [4]. This means the policy framework influenced by Supergen/Thornley was not confined to one niche technology; it applied across major parts of the UK energy and fuels system.

The low-carbon fuels dimension is also evidenced. The Supergen sustainability briefing note records that "About 4% of the UK's transport fuels are supplied from renewable fuels", with biodiesel and bioethanol dominating the mix [3]. That shows the relevance of the research to DfT-regulated transport-fuel policy as well as to heat and power.

The DESNZ public dialogue on biomass and BECCS adds further reach. It involved **about 105 members of the public**, with **95 attending all five workshops** in June-July 2022, and findings were presented to **38 policymakers** at a cross-Whitehall webinar [5]. The dialogue report was then published alongside the Biomass Strategy on **10 August 2023** [5]. This demonstrates reach into both policymaking and public-policy legitimacy processes.

### Significance

**Public policy impact:** The most concrete policy change evidenced is DESNZ's commitment, in the context of the 2023 Biomass Strategy, to develop a stronger sustainability framework for biomass, including BECCS. The Sciencewise evaluation states: "The strategy commits to developing this framework, and to consult on the details in 2024" [5]. This is significant because the issue addressed by the underpinning research was precisely the inconsistency of sustainability criteria across sectors [3][4]. The impact is therefore not just agenda-setting; it is linked to a formal government commitment to regulatory development.

**Climate-governance impact:** The CCC evidence is significant because it sits within the UK's statutory carbon-budget architecture. The CCC report states that government accepted and Parliament legislated the first three carbon budgets, and that the fourth was legislated at the level recommended by the CCC [1]. Thornley's acknowledged contribution places her expertise within a process that shaped national decarbonisation strategy at the highest level [1]. For REF purposes, this is strong significance evidence, though Aston attribution remains unresolved for 2011.

**Environmental impact:** The policy area concerns sustainable biomass use and BECCS, both central to net-zero planning. The IEA update cites UK strategic estimates that BECCS could deliver **c.-55 million tonnes of net negative emissions per annum** and reduce the cost of meeting the UK's 2050 target by **up to 1% of GDP** [2]. These are not direct impact metrics attributable to Aston, but they show why influence over biomass sustainability and BECCS policy is environmentally and strategically important.

**Cross-sector systems impact:** The 2023 strategy affects electricity, heat, transport and emerging biomass-using sectors [4]. Because the policy framework spans feedstocks, land use, sustainability criteria and BECCS, the significance lies in reducing fragmentation across sectors that had previously operated under different standards [3][4]. That is a systems-level policy effect rather than a single-programme adjustment.

Overall, the evidence supports 4-5 strong representative strands of impact: CCC carbon-budget advice [1]; BEIS/DfT engagement on enabling policy frameworks [2]; Supergen's 2020 sustainability synthesis [3]; DESNZ's 2023 Biomass Strategy and consultation commitment [5][6]; and independent corroboration of Aston/Supergen's role in the strategy process [4].

### Attribution to the Research

What is evidenced:

- Thornley is explicitly acknowledged as an expert contributor to the CCC Bioenergy Review [1].
- Supergen states that it worked closely with DESNZ and other departments to inform the Biomass Strategy, and that previous work was referenced in the strategy [6].
- The Sciencewise evaluation confirms that evidence from the biomass/BECCS dialogue fed into the strategy and that the strategy committed to a stronger sustainability framework [5].
- Independent reporting links the strategy's sustainability review to the "Aston University-based" Supergen Bioenergy Hub and names Thornley in the strategy process [4].

What still needs corroboration:

- The exact citations from the Biomass Strategy to Supergen/Thornley outputs are not provided in the extracted evidence. The case should not yet claim precise documentary citation without page/footnote proof.
- The direct causal chain from the 2020 Supergen sustainability briefing [3] to the final DESNZ framework is plausible and well supported, but still needs stronger beneficiary-authored confirmation, ideally a letter from DESNZ or the strategy team.
- DfT influence is evidenced as engagement with BEIS/DfT on biomass resource availability and policy mechanisms [2], but no downstream DfT decision or consultation text is yet supplied.

**Patricia Thornley and Aston attribution:** the only affiliation window available is a reported, unverified lead that she joined Aston in 2015. On that basis, the **2011 CCC impact should currently be treated as pre-Aston unless confirmed otherwise** [1]. The **2020-2023 impacts** fall within the likely Aston period and are therefore the strongest candidates for Aston-attributable impact [2][3][4][5][6]. If Thornley has since moved institution, no departure date is supplied; any post-departure impact would need verification of Aston's continuing institutional role before being credited to Aston. No such post-departure evidence is currently in the dataset.
